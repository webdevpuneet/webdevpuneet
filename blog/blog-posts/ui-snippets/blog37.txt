<h1>10 New Copy-Paste Landing Page Snippets: A Working Command Palette, Three Pricing Calculators That Do Real Arithmetic & Modals That Hold Real State</h1>

<p>Landing page sections are usually where interactivity goes to die — a "pricing calculator" that multiplies one number by one rate, a hero animation that plays once and never responds to anything, a modal that opens and closes and does nothing in between. The ten snippets below take the opposite position: the command palette in the hero is genuinely keyboard-navigable with grouped results and match highlighting, all three pricing widgets recompute a real bill from per-resource allowances and overage rates on every input event, and the three modals each run a small state machine — a two-month date range with hover preview, a format-and-field export picker, and a moderation report form whose validation changes depending on which reason you pick.</p>

<p>Every one is a <strong>live, interactive preview</strong> you can try right here in the article, and each exports to React, Vue, Angular or Tailwind in one click from its snippet page.</p>

<h2>What these ten get right</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Totals are derived, never tracked.</strong> The seat-mix calculator keeps no running total anywhere. Each role card carries its rate on <code>data-rate</code> and its count on <code>data-count</code>, and <code>recalcTotals()</code> re-reads both across every card on every click. There is nothing to fall out of sync, because the only place the total exists is the moment it is computed — the single most reliable pattern for any calculator with more than one input.</li>
<li><strong>Interpolated HTML is escaped at every boundary.</strong> The command palette builds its result rows as an HTML string, which is exactly where injected markup usually creeps in. Its <code>highlight()</code> function splits the text at the match index and escapes each of the three pieces — before, match, after — separately, then joins them around a <code>&lt;mark&gt;</code>. The highlight is real markup; the content around it can never be.</li>
<li><strong>One function owns every derived piece of UI state.</strong> In the export modal, the summary sentence, the export button's disabled state, and the select-all button's label are all written by a single <code>updateSummary()</code>. Splitting them across three handlers is how you end up with a button that says "Deselect all" next to a summary that says "Select at least one field" — the classic half-updated state in any picker.</li>
<li><strong>Filtering hides, it does not re-render.</strong> The integration grid never removes a tile from the DOM. It toggles two classes — one to fade and pull a non-match out of the grid's flow, one to highlight a genuine match — so restoring the full grid costs nothing and no element ever loses focus or hover state mid-search.</li>
<li><strong>Simulated activity is staggered on purpose.</strong> The plan-popularity counters each schedule their own next bump with <code>4000 + Math.random() * 7000</code> and a recursive <code>setTimeout</code>, rather than sharing one interval. Real signups arriving on three plans are not synchronised, and three numbers ticking in lockstep is the detail that gives a fake live counter away instantly.</li>
</ul>

<h2>1. Hero with Interactive Command Palette Demo</h2>

<p>A developer-tool hero with a fully working Cmd+K-style palette in the fold — type to filter across grouped commands, arrow through the results, press Enter to run the highlighted one.</p>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> <code>render()</code> filters the command array on a lowercase <code>indexOf</code> match against the command name, then walks the matches emitting a <code>.cpd-group-label</code> whenever the current command's group differs from the previous one — which is why the source array is ordered by group, and that ordering is the only reason the headings need no separate grouping pass. Keyboard handling is deliberately clamped rather than wrapping: <code>ArrowDown</code> is <code>Math.min(activeIndex + 1, currentMatches.length - 1)</code> and <code>ArrowUp</code> is <code>Math.max(activeIndex - 1, 0)</code>, so holding an arrow key parks at the end of the list instead of cycling past it. <p><strong>Best for:</strong> the hero of a developer tool or internal platform, where letting a visitor <em>use</em> the interface in the fold argues for the product better than a screenshot of it. <strong>Tip:</strong> the Escape key clears the input and blurs it rather than closing anything — if you lift the palette into a real overlay, that is the one handler to rewrite first.</p>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the rotation fades the quote text and the avatar only — never the card — which is the detail that stops the section jumping as quotes of different lengths swap in and out. <code>showQuote()</code> sets both elements to <code>opacity: 0</code>, waits 180ms for the transition to land, then writes the new text, name, role and initials before fading back in, so nothing is ever visible mid-swap. <h2>4. Hero with Company Milestone Timeline Strip</h2>

<p>A horizontal year timeline where clicking any point fills the connecting track up to it and swaps in that milestone's detail card — a company story told as a progress bar rather than a bullet list.</p>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the filled portion of the track is pure arithmetic — <code>(index / (points.length - 1)) * 100</code> as a percentage width — which means the first point leaves the track empty, the last fills it completely, and adding a sixth milestone needs no hand-tuned values anywhere. <h2>5. Role-Based Seat Mix Pricing Calculator</h2>

<p>Admin, Editor and Viewer seats each with their own rate and their own stepper, totalling to a live team price — plus a blended average per seat that most per-seat pricing pages never show.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/pricing-role-based-seat-mix-calculator/embed/" style="width:100%;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Role-Based Seat Mix Pricing Calculator — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> there is no running total held in a variable. Every stepper click writes the new count back to that card's <code>data-count</code> attribute and then calls <code>recalcTotals()</code>, which loops all three cards, reads <code>data-rate</code> and <code>data-count</code> fresh, and re-derives seats, price and average from scratch. The DOM is the single source of truth, so a mis-fired event or a count set from outside the widget still produces a correct total on the next recalculation. The average line divides only when <code>totalSeats &gt; 0</code>, which is the difference between showing <code>$0.00</code> and showing <code>NaN</code> the moment someone empties every role. <code>adjustCount()</code> clamps to <code>Math.max(0, Math.min(999, current + delta))</code> so the minus button stops at zero rather than going negative. The dimmed look of an unused role is the same idea taken one step further: it comes from a pure CSS rule, <code>.rsm-role:has(.rsm-count[data-count="0"])</code>, so the visual state reads the very same attribute the arithmetic does and needs no JavaScript to stay in step.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> any product priced per seat where the seats are not all worth the same — the calculator does the job a three-column table cannot, which is answering "what would <em>my</em> team cost?" <strong>Tip:</strong> the rates live entirely in <code>data-rate</code> in the markup, so a pricing change is an HTML edit and never a JavaScript one.</p>

<h2>6. Multi-Resource Usage Billing Simulator</h2>

<p>Three sliders — API requests, storage, bandwidth — each with its own included allowance and its own overage rate, summing with a base fee into a live estimated bill.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/pricing-multi-resource-usage-simulator/embed/" style="width:100%;height:680px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Multi-Resource Usage Billing Simulator — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the pricing model is one honest line — <code>Math.max(0, usage - included) * rate</code> — applied per resource, which is what makes the included allowance behave the way a real bill does: everything under it costs nothing, and only the excess is charged. The <code>Math.max(0, …)</code> is doing real work; without it, staying under an allowance would produce a negative cost that quietly subtracts from the other resources' charges. Each resource is read once at startup into an object holding its rate, allowance, slider and output elements, and <code>recalc()</code> then re-sums the whole bill — base fee included — on every <code>input</code> event rather than adjusting a stored subtotal. Money and units get separate formatters: <code>toLocaleString</code> with two fixed decimals for currency, and a rounded, thousands-separated integer for usage.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> usage-based products where the pricing page's real job is to convince a visitor their workload lands in the affordable band. <strong>Tip:</strong> change <code>BASE_FEE</code> and the <code>data-included</code> / <code>data-rate</code> attributes to match your plan; the arithmetic and the formatting need no changes at all.</p>

<h2>7. Pricing Cards with Live Plan Popularity Counter</h2>

<p>A three-tier pricing grid where each card carries its own "picked this in the last hour" counter, ticking independently with a pulsing activity dot.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/pricing-plan-popularity-live-counter/embed/" style="width:100%;height:700px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Pricing Cards with Live Plan Popularity Counter — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> each counter schedules its own next bump with a recursive <code>setTimeout</code> at <code>4000 + Math.random() * 7000</code> milliseconds instead of sharing a single interval — so the three cards drift apart within seconds and stay apart, which is exactly how three plans receiving real signups would behave. The increment is weighted rather than uniform: <code>Math.random() &lt; 0.7</code> adds one, otherwise two, so the sequence has the occasional double-step of real traffic without ever jumping implausibly. Each bump adds a <code>.ppc-pop-bump</code> class that is removed 600ms later, flashing the number green just long enough to catch the eye of someone not staring directly at that card. The starting numbers come from <code>data-base</code> in the markup, so the counters begin somewhere credible rather than at zero.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> a pricing page where a middle tier deserves a nudge — social proof at the point of decision rather than three paragraphs above it. <strong>Tip:</strong> wire it to real data before shipping if you can. Swap the recursive timer for a polled endpoint and keep the bump animation; a fabricated counter is a small claim, but it is still a claim.</p>

<h2>8. Booking Date Range Picker Modal</h2>

<p>Two side-by-side month calendars sharing one range selection, with live hover preview of the in-between days and a nights count computed from the actual dates.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/modal-date-range-picker/embed/" style="width:100%;height:760px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Booking Date Range Picker Modal — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the whole picker runs on a three-branch state machine in <code>selectDate()</code>. With no start (or a complete range already chosen) the click sets a new start and clears everything else; a click <em>before</em> the current start moves the start rather than rejecting the click, which is what a person means when they click backwards; anything else sets the end. Hover preview is one line of cleverness: <code>previewEnd = rangeEnd || hoverDate</code>, so the same in-range comparison renders both the committed range and the tentative one, with no second code path. Each month grid is rebuilt from <code>new Date(year, month, 1).getDay()</code> for the leading blanks and <code>new Date(year, month + 1, 0).getDate()</code> for the day count — the day-zero-of-next-month trick that gets February right in a leap year without any special casing. Nights are <code>Math.round((rangeEnd - rangeStart) / 86400000)</code>, and the rounding is deliberate: across a daylight-saving boundary the difference is 23 or 25 hours, which rounds to the one night a guest actually stayed.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> booking, rental and reservation flows, where a range picker that previews the stay as you move the cursor removes most of the guesswork from picking a checkout date. <strong>Tip:</strong> past dates are disabled at render by comparing against a <code>today</code> normalised to midnight — normalising is what stops today's own date counting as past once the clock passes midnight-plus-anything.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/modal-report-content-flag-reason/embed/" style="width:100%;height:700px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Report Content Modal with Reason Picker — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> <code>updateFormState()</code> runs the whole form from the currently selected reason. The details textarea is revealed by toggling the native <code>hidden</code> property when the reason is "Something else" — the one case where a free-text explanation is genuinely required — and stays hidden for the specific reasons that already say enough on their own. <h2>How to drop these into your project</h2>

<ol>
<li>Open any snippet and hit <strong>View / Edit Code</strong> to see the HTML, CSS and JS in separate tabs, then paste the HTML into your markup, the CSS into your stylesheet, and the JS before your closing <code>&lt;/body&gt;</code> tag — or use the one-click <strong>export to React, Vue, Angular or Tailwind</strong> right from the snippet page. All ten are dependency-free vanilla JavaScript; none of them need anything installed.</li>
<li><strong>Keep your pricing in the markup, not the code.</strong> All three calculators read their rates and allowances from <code>data-</code> attributes, which means a price change is an HTML edit that a marketer can make and a code review can read at a glance. Resist the urge to move those numbers into the JavaScript when you port the snippet — that is the change that turns a pricing update into a deploy.</li>
<li><strong>Re-derive, don't accumulate.</strong> Every widget here recomputes its totals and its summary text from scratch on each event instead of adjusting a stored value. It costs nothing at these sizes and it removes an entire category of bug, which is why the seat calculator can never disagree with its own steppers.</li>
<li><strong>Give every modal an Escape key and a backdrop click.</strong> All three modals close on both, plus their own close button. It is a handful of lines, and their absence is the single most common reason a dialog feels broken — particularly to anyone navigating without a mouse.</li>
<li><strong>Be careful with the fake live counter.</strong> The plan-popularity widget is the one snippet in this batch that asserts something about the world rather than about your product. Wire it to real signup data if you can, or reframe the label into something you can stand behind; the animation and the pulse work identically either way.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Final thought</h2>

<p>The common thread across this batch is that state is computed rather than remembered. There is no stored total in the seat calculator, no cached subtotal in the usage simulator, no "is everything selected" flag in the export modal, no separately tracked previous active point in the timeline. Every one of those values is re-derived from the DOM or from the inputs at the moment it is displayed — which is why none of these widgets can end up showing two numbers that disagree with each other.</p>
