<p>Most "scroll animation" on the web is a fade-in and a parallax layer. <h2>Why these don't feel like typical "scroll effects"</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>One number drives everything.</strong> Every scene here reduces to a single scrubbed value — 0 at the top of the section, 1 at the bottom — and derives camera position, particle placement, material state, and HUD text from it each frame. Nothing is a fired, one-way tween, which is exactly what makes scrolling back up replay the scene backwards for free.</li>
<li><strong>Physics over vibes.</strong> The gear train uses real tooth-ratio math. The hourglass scales sand piles by a cube root so volume is actually conserved. The galaxy's arms use a distance-dependent spiral angle. These aren't random spinning shapes — the motion is correct, which is what makes it read as a machine instead of decoration.</li>
<li><strong>Thousands of particles, one draw call.</strong> Where a scene needs a crowd — dust, exhaust, sand, city light streaks — it's one <code>THREE.BufferGeometry</code> with a flat <code>Float32Array</code> rendered through <code>THREE.Points</code>, never one mesh per particle. That's the difference between 9,000 particles at 60fps and a frozen tab.</li>
</ul>

<p>The camera falls toward a black hole ringed by a 5,200-particle accretion disk with real Keplerian orbital physics — inner particles genuinely lap the outer rim.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/three-scroll-black-hole/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Three.js Scroll Black Hole Approach — Free WebGL Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> each disk particle stores just its orbital parameters — radius, phase, a slight tilt — plus an angular velocity following the real Keplerian falloff, <code>ω = 14 / r^1.5</code>, so inner particles visibly outpace the rim exactly the way accretion physics demands. Positions are recomputed from those parameters every frame, never integrated, which keeps the scrub exact at any scroll speed. The event horizon itself is the cheapest mesh in the scene: a plain black <code>MeshBasicMaterial</code> sphere, which ignores all lighting by definition and reads as a literal hole in space against the glowing disk. A "time dilation" multiplier speeds the disk up to 12× as you approach, reported live in the HUD, while the camera's field of view creeps from 60° to 84° for the stretched, pulled-in feeling near the horizon.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> sci-fi promos, dark-theme portfolio centerpieces, and any "gravity" brand metaphor — aggregators, data lakes, marketplaces that pull everything toward a center. <strong>Tip:</strong> pair it above the <a href="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/three-scroll-galaxy-formation/">galaxy formation</a> snippet for a two-act cosmic scroll story.</p>

<h2>5. Three.js Scroll Gear Train Mechanism</h2>

<p>Five interlocking brass gears where scroll is literally the crank — every downstream gear rotates at its exact tooth-count ratio, counter-rotating at each mesh point like a real machine.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/three-scroll-gear-train/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Three.js Scroll Gear Train Mechanism — Free WebGL Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> each gear is built procedurally from primitives — a disc, N tooth boxes placed around the rim, a hub, and dark wedges faking spoke cutouts — so tooth count is a real parameter, not decoration. The scrubbed value isn't an abstract 0–1 progress fraction, it's the driver gear's actual angle, tweened from 0 to 4π; every downstream gear's angle is then computed by chaining <code>angle × (teethPrev / teethCurrent)</code> down the train and flipping direction at each mesh point, which is the actual kinematics of spur gears. A half-tooth phase offset on alternating gears keeps teeth interleaving cleanly through contact points instead of clipping through each other.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> engineering and manufacturing sites where a mechanism that obeys real ratios signals precision, "how it works" process sections where each gear maps to a pipeline stage, and steampunk or heritage-craft brand pages. <strong>Tip:</strong> because ratios are parameter-driven, editing any gear's tooth count updates both the geometry and the motion coherently — it doubles as a live mechanism diagram.</p>

<h2>6. Three.js Scroll Hourglass Sand Timer</h2>

<p>Sand runs through a glass with genuinely conserved volume — the top pile scales down while the bottom grows by the exact same cube-root law — then the whole instrument flips over at the end of scroll.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/three-scroll-hourglass/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Three.js Scroll Hourglass Sand Timer — Free WebGL Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> a cone scaled uniformly by <code>s</code> holds <code>s³</code> of its volume, so naively scaling pile height linearly would make sand visibly evaporate mid-run. The correct mapping is <code>scale = cbrt(fraction)</code> — the top pile shrinks by <code>cbrt(1 − run)</code> and the bottom grows by <code>cbrt(run)</code>, so their combined volume stays constant at every scroll position, and the top pile collapses dramatically near the end purely as a consequence of that math, exactly like a real hourglass. A 220-particle sand stream threads the neck with gravity-like acceleration and despawns right at the surface of the rising bottom pile. The final 12% of scroll smoothsteps the whole rig through a half-turn, resetting the timer upside down.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> deadline and countdown campaigns where urgency should visibly accelerate near the end, and heritage or slow-craft brands (watchmakers, distillers) where patience is the message. <strong>Tip:</strong> the cube-root volume law is worth stealing any time you scale a container to represent "how full" something is — linear scaling will always look wrong.</p>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> each shard starts life as a plain low-poly icosahedron, non-uniformly stretched along one axis so the same faceted base shape reads as a crystal spike instead of a round gem, with zero extra vertices. <h2>8. Three.js Scroll 3D Book Page Flip</h2>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/three-scroll-book-pages/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Three.js Scroll 3D Book Page Flip — Free WebGL Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> a page needs to rotate around the book's spine, not its own center, and the snippet gets there with one line — translating the page's <code>PlaneGeometry</code> so its left edge sits at the local origin, which turns a plain <code>rotation.z</code> into the flip itself, no pivot group required. <p><strong>Best for:</strong> publishing, author, and bookstore sites, portfolios framed as a storybook with a project per spread, and wedding or event pages built as a turning guestbook. <strong>Tip:</strong> the printed lines are children of each page mesh, so they inherit its rotation for free — swap them for a <code>CanvasTexture</code> per page if you want real page content instead of placeholder strips.</p>
