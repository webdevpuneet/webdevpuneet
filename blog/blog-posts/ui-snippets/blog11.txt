<h2>What actually makes a scroll animation work</h2>

<p>Before picking an effect, it helps to know what separates a scroll animation that feels premium from one that feels gimmicky. Regardless of which of the ten below you choose, the good ones share three traits:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The scrollbar is the playhead, not a trigger.</strong> The best scroll effects are <em>scrubbed</em> — their progress is tied directly to scroll position, so they run backward when you scroll up. That reversibility is what makes them feel physically connected to your input rather than fired once and forgotten.</li>
<li><strong>Motion serves the content.</strong> An animation that draws a line between two steps, reveals a stat as you reach it, or assembles a product as it's described earns its place. Motion with no relationship to the copy beside it is just noise.</li>
<li><strong>It stays smooth on real devices.</strong> Scrubbed effects run on every frame, so they animate cheap properties — transforms, opacity, a canvas draw — and never trigger layout on scroll.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep that in mind as you scroll — every snippet below is scroll-driven and reversible, and you can borrow the underlying technique even if you don't use the exact demo.</p>

<h2>1. Scroll Image Sequence</h2>

<p>The technique behind Apple's AirPods and MacBook pages: an animation rendered as individual frames and drawn to a canvas, with the scrollbar acting as the playhead so the sequence plays frame by frame as you scroll.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/scroll-image-sequence/embed/" style="width:100%;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Scroll Image Sequence — Free HTML/CSS/JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> a set of frames is preloaded, and GSAP's ScrollTrigger scrubs a single frame-index value from the first frame to the last as you scroll through a pinned section. Whichever frame that value lands on is painted to a <code>&lt;canvas&gt;</code> with <code>drawImage</code>, so only one image is ever on screen and the "video" is really just the right still for your scroll position. Because it's scrubbed, scrolling back up plays the sequence in reverse with no extra code.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> product reveals, hardware and device pages, and hero sections that want cinematic weight without an actual video file. <strong>Tip:</strong> render your sequence at the display size you need and preload every frame before the effect starts — the whole illusion depends on the next frame already being decoded when the scroll reaches it.</p>

<h2>2. Scroll Typewriter</h2>

<p>The classic typing animation turned into a scrubbed one: characters appear one by one as you scroll down, and un-type — caret and all — when you scroll back up.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/scroll-typewriter/embed/" style="width:100%;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Scroll Typewriter — Free HTML/CSS/JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> GSAP ScrollTrigger scrubs a character count from zero to the length of the string as you move through a pinned section, and each frame the snippet shows just that many characters with a blinking caret at the end. Nothing is on a timer, so the reversibility is free — scroll up and the value shrinks, deleting characters one at a time. It's the same scrubbing idea as the image sequence, applied to text instead of frames.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> landing-page headlines, terminal and code-tool demos, and story sections where a line should "type" into place as the reader arrives. <strong>Tip:</strong> keep the underlying text a real, complete text node and reveal a slice of it — that way it stays selectable and readable by screen readers even mid-animation.</p>

<h2>3. Scroll SVG Path Draw</h2>

<p>A winding line or route that appears to draw itself stroke by stroke as you scroll — the technique behind animated roadmaps, timelines, and "how it works" sections.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/scroll-svg-path-draw/embed/" style="width:100%;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Scroll SVG Path Draw — Free HTML/CSS/JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the SVG path is given a <code>stroke-dasharray</code> equal to its own total length, so the entire stroke is one long dash followed by an equal gap. GSAP ScrollTrigger then scrubs the <code>stroke-dashoffset</code> from the full length down to zero as you scroll, sliding the dash into view so the line appears to draw. Reverse the scroll and the offset grows again, un-drawing the path.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> process and roadmap sections, journey maps, and timelines where each waypoint should be revealed as the reader reaches it. <strong>Tip:</strong> attach labels or markers along the path and fade each one in as the draw passes it, so the line and its annotations reveal together instead of the text appearing all at once.</p>

<h2>4. Stacking Scroll Cards</h2>

<p>Full-width cards pin to the top of the viewport one after another, and as you keep scrolling the next card slides up to cover the previous one — which shrinks back like a card going into a deck.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/stacking-scroll-cards/embed/" style="width:100%;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Stacking Scroll Cards — Free HTML/CSS/JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> each card uses <code>position: sticky</code> with a top offset, so it sticks to the top of the viewport while its section scrolls past, and the next sticky card rides up over it. A small scale-down on the outgoing card makes it recede like it's slipping into a stack rather than being flatly hidden. The whole effect is CSS-first — no library needed for the core stacking.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> feature walkthroughs, step-by-step storytelling, and pricing or plan comparisons where each card deserves a full moment on screen. <strong>Tip:</strong> give each card a slightly different top offset so a sliver of the previous card peeks out above the current one — that visible edge is what reads as a real physical stack.</p>

<h2>5. Scrollytelling Chart</h2>

<p>The data-journalism pattern popularized by the New York Times and The Pudding: narrative steps scroll up one column while a sticky chart beside them morphs to illustrate each step, so the reader controls the pace and the chart always matches the paragraph in view.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/scroll-story-chart/embed/" style="width:100%;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Scrollytelling Chart — Free HTML/CSS/JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the chart sits in a <code>position: sticky</code> column so it stays fixed on screen while the text steps scroll past it. Each step is tracked as it scrolls into view, and as one becomes active the chart transitions to that step's data — bars regrow, a line redraws, a series highlights. Because the active step is derived from scroll position, scrolling back up returns the chart to the previous state exactly.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> data stories, annual reports, and explainer sections where numbers need narration rather than a static chart the reader has to interpret alone. <strong>Tip:</strong> animate only between the states you actually narrate — a chart that morphs on every pixel of scroll is distracting; one that settles clearly on each step's data is readable.</p>

<h2>6. Lottie Scroll Scrub</h2>

<p>A Lottie animation played with the scrollbar instead of a clock: as you scroll a pinned section, a progress ring draws itself around a circle and a checkmark strokes in at the end, rewinding when you scroll back up.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/lottie-scroll-scrub/embed/" style="width:100%;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Lottie Scroll Scrub — Free HTML/CSS/JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the animation loads with <code>autoplay: false</code> so its internal timer never runs, and GSAP ScrollTrigger scrubs a 0-to-1 value that is mapped onto the animation's frame range. Every frame the snippet calls <code>goToAndStop(frame, true)</code> to seek that exact frame, so scroll position — not time — decides what's on screen. The drawing effect itself is a Lottie trim path animating from 0 to 100 percent.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> scroll-to-complete progress, onboarding steps, and any section where a designer-made vector animation should advance in step with the copy. <strong>Tip:</strong> the animation here is inline JSON, but you can point the player at your own exported <code>.json</code> file and the scroll logic stays identical.</p>

<h2>Keep your scroll animations fast and accessible</h2>

<p>A scroll effect that looks great but janks the page or excludes users isn't doing its job. Three rules cover almost every case:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Scrub, don't fire-and-forget.</strong> Tie progress to scroll position (a numeric ScrollTrigger scrub, or seeking a frame) rather than starting a fixed-duration animation on entry — that's what makes an effect reversible and feel connected to the user's input.</li>
<li><strong>Respect <code>prefers-reduced-motion</code>.</strong> For heavy 3D or fast-moving effects, offer a reduced or static fallback for visitors who've asked for less motion, and never trap them in a long pinned section they can't scroll past.</li>
<li><strong>Clean up on unmount.</strong> In a framework, kill ScrollTrigger instances and call <code>renderer.dispose()</code> or <code>anim.destroy()</code> when the component leaves, so pins, WebGL contexts, and scroll listeners don't leak between pages.</li>
</ul>
