<h2>1. Canvas Metaball Blobs</h2>

<p>Soft, gooey blobs that merge and split with a wet, organic pull as they drift and respond to the cursor — a true scalar field sampled and rasterized directly into pixel data, no WebGL, no shader.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/canvas-metaball-blobs/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Canvas Metaball Blobs — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> for every point on a coarse sampling grid, <code>render()</code> sums each ball's contribution as <code>radius² / distance²</code> — an inverse-square falloff, the same shape used for gravitational fields — into a shared scalar field. Where that summed field crosses a threshold of <code>1.0</code>, the pixel is "inside" the merged shape; nothing special-cases the merge itself, it's a direct, automatic consequence of two nearby balls' fields overlapping and pushing the combined value over threshold across a wider connected region. Sampling every device pixel at 60fps would be far too slow, so the field is computed on a coarse grid instead and each cell filled as a small flat block, trading a little edge smoothness for real-time performance. The cursor doesn't repel the blobs — within 220px, <code>updateBalls()</code> nudges each ball's velocity toward the pointer by a small constant amount, a deliberately non-physical "wet pull" rather than an accurate force.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> hero backgrounds, loading screens, and creative-agency sites where an organic, liquid ambient layer reads as more alive than a static gradient. <strong>Tip:</strong> reduce the sampling <code>cell</code> size for finer, smoother edges at the cost of per-frame work, and compare it with <a href="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/canvas-water-ripple-simulation/">Canvas Water Ripple Simulation</a> below for a very different flavor of grid-based canvas simulation.</p>

<h2>2. Canvas Boids Flocking Simulation</h2>

<p>A real-time flock of a hundred-plus boids that align, cluster, and avoid collisions with no leader and no central coordination — pure emergent behavior from Craig Reynolds' three classic 1986 steering rules, with the cursor acting as a predator.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/canvas-boids-flocking/embed/" style="width:100%;height:600px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Canvas Boids Flocking Simulation — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> every frame, <code>flock()</code> looks only at neighbors within a fixed 55px <code>PERCEPTION</code> radius and computes three steering vectors — <strong>separation</strong> (steer away, weighted more strongly the closer a neighbor is), <strong>alignment</strong> (steer toward the average heading of nearby boids), and <strong>cohesion</strong> (steer toward their average position). No boid has any awareness of the flock as a whole; it only ever reacts to whoever happens to be within its own radius. The three vectors aren't averaged equally — separation is weighted roughly 1.6x, alignment 1.0x, cohesion 0.9x — which is the single biggest lever over whether the result reads as a tight flock, a loose swarm, or a scattered mess. Both the individual steering forces and each boid's final velocity pass through a <code>limit()</code> helper that caps magnitude while preserving direction, keeping turns smooth instead of jittery. The cursor predator is just a fourth force summed in on top of the other three: any boid within 120px steers directly away from the pointer.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> AI/simulation teaching demos, data or nature-themed landing pages, and interactive portfolio pieces that want to demonstrate real algorithmic depth rather than a looping video. <strong>Tip:</strong> the neighbor search is a naive O(n²) scan, intentionally — fine up to a few hundred boids, but worth bucketing into a spatial grid before pushing into the thousands.</p>

<h2>3. Canvas Water Ripple Simulation</h2>

<p>Click, drag, or touch the surface and real waves propagate outward and interfere with each other — an actual discretized 2D wave equation stepped forward on a height-field grid every frame, not a decorative expanding circle.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/canvas-water-ripple-simulation/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Canvas Water Ripple Simulation — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> the water surface is two <code>Float32Array</code> grids, <code>current</code> and <code>previous</code>. Every frame, <code>stepWave()</code> takes the sum of its four neighbors' previous heights, halves it, and subtracts the cell's own existing value — <code>(neighborSum / 2 - current[i])</code>, the discrete form of <code>2 × average − old height</code> — then damps the result by a constant just under 1. That's the standard finite-difference discretization of the wave equation, the same family of numerical method used in real fluid and acoustic simulation. Because every disturbance feeds into that same shared grid, overlapping ripples genuinely add together and interfere rather than just drawing on top of each other. Damping is what makes the surface visibly settle back to stillness instead of oscillating forever, compounding a small energy loss every single step. The clever part is the shading: instead of mapping height directly to color, <code>render()</code> computes the local height gradient between neighboring cells and uses that gradient — not the raw height — to brighten or darken the base water color, approximating how a real surface refracts light depending on its slope, which is what gives flat ripples a convincing sense of depth.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> physics teaching demos, relaxation/wellness apps, and interactive hero sections that want a genuinely tactile, physically-grounded background rather than a loop. <strong>Tip:</strong> pair the technique conceptually with <a href="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/canvas-metaball-blobs/">Canvas Metaball Blobs</a> above — both rasterize a computed scalar field directly into <code>ImageData</code>, just from a very different underlying simulation.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/glb-configurator-color-swatches/embed/" style="width:100%;height:620px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="GLB Product Color Configurator — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> a glTF scene graph frequently reuses one material instance across several mesh nodes to keep the file small — set <code>.color</code> directly on that shared material and every mesh using it recolors, not just the one you meant to change. The moment the model loads, this snippet walks its full node tree with <code>car.traverse()</code> and clones every mesh's material with <code>node.material.clone()</code> before collecting the clones into a <code>paintMaterials</code> array; every swatch and finish click afterward only ever touches those per-instance clones. Color and finish are two genuinely independent controls: swatches set <code>.color</code>, finish presets set <code>.metalness</code>/<code>.roughness</code> together, so any of six colors combines correctly with any of three finishes with zero extra state to track.</p>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> most exploded diagrams get built by hand, one offset per part. <h2>9. Text Diff Checker</h2>

<p>Paste two versions of a paragraph and see exactly which words were removed and added, computed by a real longest-common-subsequence algorithm running entirely client-side — no diff-match-patch, no external library.</p>

<iframe src="https://fwdtools.com/ui-snippets/text-diff-checker/embed/" style="width:100%;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;overflow:hidden;" loading="lazy" title="Text Diff Checker — Free HTML CSS JS Snippet by FWD Tools"></iframe>

<p><strong>How it works:</strong> <code>tokenize()</code> splits on <code>/(\s+)/</code> with a capturing group, which — unlike a plain whitespace split — keeps every run of spaces as its own token, so the diff can tell "quick brown" and "quick  brown" apart and never has to guess where spacing goes on reassembly. <code>diffWords()</code> then builds a dynamic-programming table where <code>dp[i][j]</code> holds the length of the longest common subsequence between the tails of both token arrays starting at <code>i</code> and <code>j</code> — the same core recurrence <code>git diff</code> and most real diff tools use. A forward walk from <code>(0, 0)</code> reconstructs the actual edits: matching tokens are equal, and at a mismatch the walk follows whichever neighboring cell in the table holds the larger value, deciding whether that token was deleted or inserted. Deleted and inserted tokens render inside real <code>&lt;del&gt;</code> and <code>&lt;ins&gt;</code> elements — the semantically correct tags, not styled <code>&lt;span&gt;</code>s — with every token passed through <code>escapeHtml()</code> first so pasted angle brackets can't break the output.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> contract and legal document review, copyediting tools, and translation QA — anywhere a word-level "what actually changed" answer matters more than a raw text dump. <strong>Tip:</strong> the same <code>diffWords()</code> function works at character granularity too — drop the whitespace-preserving regex and split on empty string for finer-grained diffs within a single changed word.</p>
